AI Research Workspace

A workspace for reading twelve documents at once and coming out the other end with a decision, not another PDF to read later.

Type
Self-initiated product
Role
Product Designer
Timeline
10 Weeks
Focus
Information architecture, synthesis UX

Overview

Real research questions almost never have one document's worth of answer. They have twelve. And the interesting bit is usually where those twelve disagree with each other. Most tools either summarise the documents one at a time or squash the whole set into one answer that hides where anything came from.

This workspace makes reading across documents the main thing, not a feature. Built for the people who read to decide, not just read.

Problem

The way most people compare sources doesn't work past about three or four:

  • You end up with too many tabs and a mental model held together with vibes.
  • Highlights and notes split across different tools and never end up in the same place.
  • AI summaries blend everything into a false middle. The disagreements, which are usually the whole point, vanish.
  • Answers come with no citations, so you re-check them anyway. You're back to reading.

The Goal

Let one person hold twelve documents in their head at once. Every claim in the synthesis links back to the passage that supports it.

How I'd measure it

A self-initiated concept, not a shipped product, so there are no live numbers. If it shipped, these are the signals I'd track and what good would look like.

Time-to-decision
Question to defensible answer, vs the tabs-and-notes workflow it replaces
Traceability
Share of synthesised claims a user can trace to a source passage in one click
Trust
Do people stop re-checking every line, measured by how often they open the source

Design Challenges

Reading a lot, quickly, without losing the parts that matter.

01
Sixty cells that don't drown you

Twelve documents against five questions is a sixty-cell grid. That's a lot to look at. Each cell shows a one-line stance (agree, disagree, silent) with the full passage a click away. You scan the grid to find the interesting rows, then dive into the cells that matter.

02
Making disagreement the point, not a footnote

Most AI synthesis flattens everything into a middle. The tool does the opposite. Cells where the sources actually disagree get highlighted. Those are the rows that will change your mind, so they're what the tool draws you toward.

Design Solutions

Three ideas that make the workspace feel less like reading and more like thinking.

01
Feature

The claim matrix

Documents across the top, your questions down the side. Every cell tells you where that source stands. Reading becomes scanning. The gaps in the evidence, which used to hide across twelve PDFs, are suddenly obvious.

02
Feature

Every claim comes with its receipts

The summary is generated, but every sentence in it links back to the passages behind it. Confidence tracks how many sources agree. If you don't trust a line, you can check it in one click.

03
Feature

You get a decision brief, not a summary

The end of the workflow isn't a paragraph of prose. It's a one-pager: the question, what the evidence says, where the sources disagree, and a recommendation. Built to be forwarded to whoever's actually making the call.

Key Learnings

What working on this changed my mind about.

Half the synthesis work is a layout problem

The matrix did a lot of the work before any AI ran. Getting the structure right first meant the generation had less to do, and what it did do was easier to verify.

Citations are the interface, not metadata

Once every claim had a visible source, people stopped re-checking everything and started skimming with confidence. Trust turns out to be an interaction pattern, not a personality trait.

Design for the decision, not the document

The workspace only started feeling coherent once the output became a decision brief. Framing the end state that way pulled every earlier screen into focus.
