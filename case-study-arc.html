Design Systems · Upstox

Arc Design System

Upstox's design system had 800+ colour tokens and designers spending more time fighting it than using it. We rebuilt Arc around one idea: designers don't need tokens, they need decisions.

Challenge

Arc had grown into 800+ colour tokens across three layers and per-component files that designers couldn't even find. Designing felt like debugging, and every page looked like a slightly different product.

Move

Collapse the token model to one semantic layer, consolidate 32 components into four cross-platform libraries, and wire Figma straight into code so tokens and icons can never drift again.

Result

One source of truth across Android, iOS and Web; a one-click Figma-to-repo pipeline that saves 20+ minutes on every update; and a shift from a gatekeeping DS team to an open contribution model.

Overview

Arc is the design system behind the whole Upstox ecosystem: trading, investing, mutual funds, IPO, insurance, loans, wealth. It started life in 2024 as a set of Figma files called “design-system-v2”. By late 2025 it had scaled past the point where those files could keep up.

This is a team story, and I want to be honest about that up front: Arc is the work of a design-systems pod and a platform engineering group. My part was the token simplification, a chunk of the components and icons, the accessibility fixes, and most of the migration communication that kept 40-plus production files from breaking. Where I say “we”, I mean the team.

Problem

The system had three problems tangled together:

  • Token overload. 800+ colour tokens, roughly 250 just for backgrounds. Designers spent more time choosing tokens than designing, and still paired them wrong.
  • Fragmentation. Per-component Figma libraries that, in one architect's words, “aren't even discoverable, so designers can't use them unless they copy old screens.” Nested instances lagged people's laptops.
  • Drift. With no shared decision layer, every designer applied their own taste. Home, Holdings and Portfolio started looking like they belonged to different families.

Why This Problem Mattered

A design system that's hard to use doesn't just slow designers down. It quietly breaks the product. Wrong token pairings failed accessibility. Broken components blocked releases. And the whole team leaned on the DS pod for answers, which made the pod the bottleneck. As I put it in the internal deck: designing shouldn't feel like debugging. That's a system failure, not a people problem.

Research & Discovery

Before touching a single token, we audited what we actually had and benchmarked it against the systems we respect.

Inventory
The token & component audit
What we found

A full count of colour, metric and typography variables plus every component across the fragmented libraries. The headline: 800+ colour tokens, ~250 for backgrounds alone, and ~32 components spread across files nobody could reliably find.

Benchmark
How the best systems handle this
What we studied

Material 3, Fluent 2, Atlassian, Polaris and Apple HIG / SAP Fiori. The elevation study alone compared Arc's scale against five of them and confirmed none use negative-Y shadows, which settled a live internal debate.

Platform data
Can we even ship letter-spacing?
What the data said

A backward-support analysis on order-placing users (301.8k on Android, 296.6k on iOS) showed 100% on API/OS versions that support native letterSpacing. Evidence, not assumption, unblocked a typography decision.

Design Principles

Six principles the whole pod could point at when a decision got contentious. The first one is the one I kept coming back to.

01
Surface decisions, not variables

Designers don't need 800 tokens. They need to know which one is right here. The system should carry the complexity so the designer carries the decision.

02
Consistency over customization

A shared language beats individual taste. If two designers can make the same page look like two products, the system, not the designers, has failed.

03
Accessibility by default

Contrast and touch targets are baked into the tokens and components, not bolted on at review. A wrong pairing shouldn't be possible to pick.

04
Token-first, components before screens

Every visual value flows through a token; every screen is built from components. No hard-coded hex, no one-off layouts.

05
Documentation is part of the product

A component isn't done when it's drawn. It's done when someone else can use it without asking us.

06
Govern, don't gatekeep

Rules exist to keep the system lean, not to make the DS pod a bottleneck. The end state is shared ownership, not a locked door.

Foundations

The layer everything else inherits from, and where most of the real work happened.

Tokens: three layers down to one

The legacy model was L0 primitives (raw hex) → L1 semantic (“surface-primary”) → L2 component-scoped tokens. Powerful, but it meant designers were reasoning about three layers to place one colour. We designed L1v2: a single semantic layer designers actually touch, with the system resolving the rest internally. On release, the old L2 tokens went private and we watched for a month before migrating. There was no clean-cut switch that could break live work.

Typography: Messina Sans to Inter

We migrated brand and product from Messina Sans to Inter for consistency and availability. It wasn't free. Inter's high x-height can read “childish” at large sizes, so the plan added Inter Display for 20pt-and-up and letter-spacing tuning. Naming also moved from t-shirt sizes to intent-based names (Heading-1, Body-Default, Meta-Label) so platform values can differ while the intent stays constant.

Iconography, elevation, motion

Icons migrated to the Huge Icons library on a strict 24×24 grid with 1.5px strokes, keeping the same names so nothing downstream broke. Elevation became a seven-level scale on a Y×2=Blur rule with no negative-Y shadows, a decision the benchmark study made for us. Motion got its own system: easing and duration tokens plus a cross-platform handoff doc, so animation stopped being per-designer improvisation.

Dark mode & theming

Light and dark resolve automatically through alias rebinds, so designers work in one place and both themes stay correct, once we'd fixed the contrast failures the old token pairings were producing.

Design Challenges

Three tensions that shaped every decision.

01
Simplify the model without losing the power

The three-layer token model was genuinely useful. At one point it revealed the exact blast radius of a component change. Collapsing to one semantic layer for designers couldn't mean throwing that away; the complexity had to move inside the system, not disappear. That tension (is L2 the wrong abstraction, or does L1 just need better categorisation?) was still a live, healthy debate on the team.

02
Migrate 41 live files without breaking anyone

Engineers referenced 41 production files through Code Connect. Change a node-id and their build breaks. We couldn't freeze the product for a design-system rebuild, so migration had to happen underneath live work, which meant tolerating messy overlap instead of a clean switch.

03
Keep the system lean as everyone wanted in

Once the system worked, every team wanted their pattern in it. Left unchecked, the global library becomes a dumping ground. We needed a way to say “not yet” that felt fair rather than territorial: governance that scaled contribution instead of blocking it.

Design Solutions

The decisions that did the heavy lifting.

Constraints we designed inside

The product could not stop shipping during the rebuild. Engineering demanded traceability, so every change ran through a Jira ticket. Code Connect was fragile. Node-ids couldn't change casually. And the mandate covered three platforms (Android, iOS, Web) that each build tokens differently. Everything below had to survive those four facts at once.

01
Tokens

One semantic layer, L1v2

Designers now interact with a single semantic layer. The old primitives and component tokens still exist. They just live below the waterline where the system resolves them.

Decision

Introduce an L1v2 semantic layer and make the old L2 tokens private on release rather than deleting them.

Why

Token overload was the root problem, but a hard delete would break in-flight production designs. Going private, then observing for a month, let us simplify the designer's surface without a big-bang migration.

Rejected

Keeping the full three-layer model exposed. It kept designers “debugging” tokens instead of designing.

02
Architecture

Separate Mobile and Web systems

Rather than one shared cross-platform system, Arc runs as platform-specific libraries (Mobile Components, Web Components, and matching Patterns files), with designers owning UX consistency across them.

Decision

Keep the mobile and web design systems separate, consolidated into four files instead of dozens of per-component libraries.

Why

Platform-specific systems are easier to build and maintain, and Code Connect works cleanly when a web build references web components. Mobile components were being used on web, which had broken that link.

Rejected

A single unified system. Elegant on paper, but it forced compromises neither platform's engineers wanted to maintain.

03
Automation

One-click Figma-to-code sync

Tokens and icons now flow from Figma to the repo through plugins and an internal service that opens the pull request. A designer can trigger a sync from Figma with no engineer in the loop.

Decision

Automate the whole token/icon pipeline instead of syncing by hand.

Why

The manual path meant downloading ~12 files, running a local sync, then raising and merging a PR. It was slow and it drifted. As the engineer who built it put it: “now it's one click and a few minutes, even designers can trigger it themselves.” That's the 20+ minutes saved on every update.

Guardrail

A screenshot-regression suite (1000+ tests per run) gates every sync, so “automated” never means “unverified.”

04
Patterns

The “3+ Rule” graduation gate

A local, vertical-specific pattern only graduates into the global system once three or more verticals actually use it. Until then it lives in that team's own pattern file.

Decision

Split Component vs Pattern, and put a usage-based promotion gate between local patterns and the global DS.

Why

It answers “can my thing go in the system?” with evidence instead of politics, and keeps the global library from becoming a dumping ground.

05
Mobile

Bottom sheets over dropdowns

On mobile, the system prefers bottom sheets to dropdown menus by default: bigger tap areas, better readability, and alignment with native iOS and Android behaviour. A small decision, but the kind the system now makes for you so every designer doesn't re-litigate it.

Components in practice

A system is only as real as its components. A few that show how requests turned into durable, token-driven parts.

Slots
Composable by default

Slots became a major capability push, letting complex components accept arbitrary content instead of shipping ten near-identical variants. Backed by a research report and a designer workshop so the pattern was understood, not just available.

FAB & Chips
Variants driven by real requests

Selected states, counter badges, icon-only variants: each added because a product team hit a real wall, each expressed as tokens (like button/fab/*/selected) so the change propagated everywhere at once.

Table & Toast
Fixing the quiet bugs

A table hover state that shared a token with inactive rows got its own moderate/intense steps; a toast's weight, stroke and line-height were corrected. Unglamorous, but this is what “consistent” actually costs.

Charts
Colour that stays legible

Dedicated chart colour variables (a high-contrast line-chart set versus shared background colours for other chart types), so multi-line and small charts stay readable where general UI colours would fail.

How it unfolded

Arc didn't arrive in one release. It moved through phases, each one earning the next.

2024
Scattered files

“design-system-v2” exists as Figma files. Useful, but fragmented. The starting point, not a system.

Mid 2025
A real pod forms

Dedicated DS designer and engineering channels, a success-metrics framework, and per-component VQA. The system gets owners.

Late 2025
Web, motion, type

Web DS readiness, a motion system POC, and the Messina→Inter typography migration land in parallel.

Early 2026
The re-architecture

A dedicated architect joins; the team aligns on separate mobile/web systems, the L1v2 token plan, and a step-by-step migration with focus-group validation.

Mid 2026
Consolidate & automate

Four consolidated libraries ship, Huge Icons migrates, accessibility token fixes merge to main, and the one-click Figma-to-code pipeline goes live.

Documentation & Governance

The part that decides whether a design system lives or rots.

Intake that reduces misunderstanding

The old Slack-bot request form was “causing a lot of discrepancies in understanding.” I replaced it with call-based scoping, a short conversation to actually understand the request before anything gets built. Every change then runs through a Jira ticket, which gave engineering the traceability they'd asked for.

Review discipline

Exploration files stopped going to review: only production-ready branch files, with components living in the right local library. Change freezes were used deliberately: once engineering finished a set of components, further changes only happened if they were release-blocking. “Do not chase idealism” became a useful phrase.

From gatekeeping to contribution

The biggest governance shift was philosophical: from a centralized DS team that owned everything to an open contribution model where feature designers own their pattern libraries and the “3+ Rule” decides what graduates to global. Documentation moved toward a “to the point, no BS” bar, the standard we admired in systems like Wise.

Collaboration

Arc worked because it was treated as a shared product, not a design deliverable.

Design × Engineering
Co-designed, not handed off

The deepest axis. Engineers and designers aligned on architecture together, co-designed the token generator and Code Connect setup, and engineering built the automation pipeline on our input. Screenshot tests were the shared QA loop.

Design × Product designers
Validated before rollout

A focus group of named product designers stress-tested L1v2 in ~30-minute usability sessions. New designers were checked for whether they understood the decision framework, not just the components.

Design × Brand
One typographic voice

Typography had to line up across Adobe (brand) and Figma (product). We kept a running alignment so the marketing site and the product read as the same company, without letting marketing needs overpopulate the product system.

Key Learnings

What building Arc taught me about design systems.

A design system's job is to remove decisions, not add options

800 tokens felt thorough. It was actually a tax. The system got better the moment we started hiding complexity instead of exposing it. Designers need decisions, not variables.

Governance is as important as components

The “3+ Rule” and the contribution model did more for the system's health than any single component. Without a way to say “not yet,” a good library rots into a dumping ground.

Automation is what makes consistency stick

You can preach “single source of truth” forever, but drift is a manual-process problem. Wiring Figma into the repo is what actually made the promise true and gave designers back 20 minutes an update.

Migrate underneath live work, not instead of it

Tolerating messy overlap (two Code Connect links, a month of private tokens) was uncomfortable but correct. A clean big-bang cutover would have broken 41 files and the team's trust with them.
